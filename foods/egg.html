Egg Health Benefits

Choline — The Most Important Nutrient in Eggs

Eggs are the most concentrated common dietary source of choline, providing 293mg per 100g (53% DV) — primarily in the yolk. Choline is essential for brain development during pregnancy, liver function, nerve signal transmission via acetylcholine, and cell membrane integrity. An estimated 90% of Americans are choline-deficient. Choline deficiency during pregnancy impairs fetal brain development — yet eggs are one of the most effective dietary solutions. The Institute of Medicine recommends 450mg/day for pregnant women — two large eggs provide the majority of this requirement.

Complete Protein — PDCAAS 1.0

Eggs contain all 9 essential amino acids in proportions that precisely match human needs — achieving the maximum PDCAAS (protein quality score) of 1.0. This is why eggs have long been the reference standard against which all other protein foods are compared. The amino acid profile is particularly rich in leucine (triggering muscle protein synthesis), methionine (liver health), and tryptophan (serotonin production). Egg protein is also among the most digestible — bioavailability of cooked egg protein is ~91%.

Eye Health — Lutein and Zeaxanthin

Egg yolks are rich in lutein and zeaxanthin — carotenoids that accumulate in the macula of the retina and act as natural blue-light filters. Although the absolute amounts are less than in some vegetables, the lutein and zeaxanthin in egg yolks have significantly higher bioavailability (up to 3× more absorbable) than from plant sources because they are packaged with fat in the yolk. Regular egg consumption is associated with reduced risk of age-related macular degeneration and cataracts.

Cholesterol Rehabilitation

For decades, eggs were incorrectly blamed for cardiovascular disease due to their cholesterol content. Multiple large meta-analyses (including a 2020 analysis of 33 studies covering 1.7 million people) have found no significant association between egg consumption and cardiovascular disease in healthy people. For most people, dietary cholesterol has minimal impact on blood cholesterol because the liver compensates by reducing its own production. Eggs' saturated fat content (1.9g/egg) is more relevant than their cholesterol for cardiovascular risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many calories in an egg?
One large egg (50g) has 78 calories. Per 100g: 155 calories. Two large eggs: 156 calories. The yolk has ~55 kcal and the white ~17 kcal.
Are eggs healthy?
Yes — eggs are one of the most nutritious foods. They provide complete protein, choline (53% DV), B12 (46% DV), and are rich in lutein for eye health. Large meta-analyses show egg consumption is not associated with cardiovascular disease in healthy people.
Is egg yolk bad for cholesterol?
No — dietary cholesterol from eggs has minimal impact on blood cholesterol for most people. Large meta-analyses of 100,000+ people show no link between moderate egg consumption and heart disease. The yolk contains the majority of vitamins, choline, and healthy fats.
How many eggs per day is safe?
Most health authorities consider 1-2 eggs per day safe for healthy adults. Some studies show up to 3 eggs/day have no adverse effect on lipids. People with diabetes or cardiovascular disease should discuss with their doctor.
Are eggs keto?
Yes — 1.12g carbs per 100g. Eggs are one of the most popular keto foods due to their high fat and protein content with near-zero carbs.
Boiled vs fried egg calories?
Boiled/poached: ~78 kcal per large egg. Fried in butter: ~108 kcal. Scrambled with milk: ~90 kcal. The egg itself is the same — added fats change the total.
