Quick summary: Pumpkin contains just 26 calories per 100g. It provides 344% of daily vitamin A (from beta-carotene) and significant potassium (340mg/100g) for cardiovascular health. With 92% water content and only 6.0g net carbs per 100g, pumpkin is suitable for keto and all low-calorie diets. Pumpkin seeds are a separate nutritional powerhouse with 30g protein and exceptional zinc content.

Pumpkin Health Benefits & Nutrition

Beta-Carotene and Immune Health

Pumpkin's orange colour comes from beta-carotene — a provitamin A carotenoid that converts to vitamin A as needed. Pumpkin provides 3,100µg of beta-carotene per 100g — 344% of the daily vitamin A requirement. This makes pumpkin the third richest common dietary source of beta-carotene after sweet potato and carrot. Vitamin A supports immune function by maintaining the integrity of skin and mucous membranes (the first barrier against pathogens) and is essential for the production of immune cells.

Eye Health Protection

Beyond beta-carotene, pumpkin contains lutein and zeaxanthin — carotenoids that protect the macula against blue light damage and age-related macular degeneration. The combination of multiple carotenoids in pumpkin (alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, lutein, zeaxanthin) provides broader eye protection than single-carotenoid sources. All carotenoids in pumpkin are fat-soluble — consuming pumpkin with olive oil or butter significantly increases their absorption.

Heart Health — Potassium and Fibre

A cup of cooked pumpkin provides 564mg of potassium — 16% of the daily requirement. High potassium diets are consistently associated with lower blood pressure and reduced cardiovascular disease risk. Pumpkin also contains phytosterols that reduce cholesterol absorption, and fiber that binds bile acids. Regular consumption of pumpkin is associated with improved lipid profiles in clinical studies.

Pumpkin Seeds — A Nutritional Powerhouse

Pumpkin seeds (pepitas) are dramatically more nutrient-dense than pumpkin flesh: 446 kcal per 100g, 30g protein, 6g zinc (54% DV), and 168mg magnesium (40% DV). They are one of the best plant sources of zinc and contain tryptophan (precursor to serotonin and melatonin). A 2011 study found that pumpkin seed extract significantly reduced benign prostatic hyperplasia symptoms. Don't discard pumpkin seeds — roast them with spices for maximum benefit.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many calories in pumpkin?
Pumpkin contains 26 calories per 100g raw. A cup of cooked mashed pumpkin (245g) has 49 calories. Canned pumpkin puree has about 83 kcal per 100g. Use the slider above for any weight.
Is pumpkin good for weight loss?
Yes — raw pumpkin is excellent for weight loss at only 26 kcal per 100g. Even cooked pumpkin is very low calorie. It is rich in beta-carotene and potassium, making it one of the most nutritious low-calorie vegetables available.
Is canned pumpkin as healthy as fresh?
Yes — canned pumpkin is actually more concentrated in nutrients because water is removed during processing. Pure canned pumpkin (not pumpkin pie filling) has more beta-carotene and fiber per serving than fresh. Check the label: it should contain only pumpkin.
Are pumpkin seeds healthy?
Extremely. Pumpkin seeds are far more nutritious than pumpkin flesh: high in protein (30g/100g), zinc, magnesium, and tryptophan. They support sleep, prostate health, and immune function. A 28g handful provides 5g of protein and significant minerals.
Is pumpkin keto?
Raw pumpkin has 6.0g net carbs per 100g — borderline for keto. In small portions (½ cup = ~3.5g net carbs), pumpkin can fit into a ketogenic diet. Canned pumpkin has higher carbs. Moderate portions are generally acceptable.
What is the difference between pumpkin and butternut squash?
Both are winter squashes with similar nutritional profiles. Butternut squash is slightly higher in calories and sweeter. Pumpkin has more beta-carotene per 100g. Both are excellent sources of vitamin A and potassium and can be used interchangeably in most recipes.
