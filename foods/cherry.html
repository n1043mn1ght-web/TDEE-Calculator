Cherry Health Benefits & Nutrition

Natural Melatonin — the Sleep Hormone

Cherries are one of the few significant dietary sources of melatonin — the hormone that regulates the sleep-wake cycle. Tart cherries (Montmorency variety) contain approximately 0.013mg of melatonin per 100g. Multiple randomised trials show that tart cherry juice consumption increases urine melatonin levels significantly and improves sleep duration by 34–84 minutes and sleep efficiency by 5–6%. This makes cherries unique among fruits in having direct, mechanistic evidence for improving sleep quality.

Post-Exercise Recovery and Muscle Soreness

Cherry juice is one of the most studied natural sports recovery aids. A landmark trial with marathon runners found that those who drank tart cherry juice for 5 days before and 2 days after a marathon had significantly less muscle soreness and strength loss. Multiple subsequent studies confirmed 20–25% reductions in exercise-induced muscle damage markers. The mechanism involves anthocyanins and quercetin inhibiting COX enzymes — essentially a natural anti-inflammatory effect similar to ibuprofen.

Gout and Uric Acid Reduction

Cherries have strong evidence for reducing gout attacks. A 2012 Boston University case-crossover study of 633 gout patients found that cherry consumption over 2 days reduced gout attack risk by 35%. Eating cherries in combination with allopurinol (gout medication) reduced risk by 75%. The mechanism involves anthocyanins inhibiting uric acid production by reducing xanthine oxidase activity.

Cardiovascular Benefits

Cherries are one of the better fruit sources of potassium (222mg/100g), which supports blood pressure regulation. Their anthocyanins reduce LDL oxidation and improve endothelial function. A 2019 study found that daily cherry consumption for 12 weeks reduced systolic blood pressure by 3 mmHg and triglycerides by 11% in older adults.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many calories in cherries?
Cherries contain 50 calories per 100g. A cup of cherries (154g) has 97 calories. A single cherry (8g, pitted) has about 4 calories. Use the slider above to calculate for any weight.
Do cherries help with sleep?
Yes — clinical evidence supports this. Cherries are one of the few foods that naturally contain melatonin. Multiple randomised trials found that tart cherry juice consumption increased sleep duration by 34–84 minutes and sleep efficiency by 5–6%.
Are cherries good for gout?
Yes — cherries have strong evidence for reducing gout attacks. Evidence suggests cherry consumption reduces gout risk by 35%, and eating cherries with gout medication reduces risk by up to 75%. Anthocyanins inhibit uric acid production.
Are cherries keto?
Generally no. Sweet cherries have 11.2g of net carbs per 100g, making them unsuitable for strict keto. A small serving (10 cherries, ~80g) provides 9g of net carbs.
What is the difference between sweet and tart cherries?
Sweet cherries (Bing, Rainier) are larger, darker, sweeter (~12g sugar/100g). Tart cherries (Montmorency, Morello) are smaller, more acidic, with less sugar (~10g/100g) and 2–4× more anthocyanins and significantly more melatonin. Tart cherries are superior for the anti-inflammatory and sleep benefits studied in research.
Can cherry juice replace whole cherries?
For sleep and recovery benefits, tart cherry juice has been the most studied form. However, juice removes fiber and concentrates sugars. For general nutrition, whole cherries are superior. If using juice, choose 100% tart cherry with no added sugar.
